# Design principles

How re-kid's games should feel and what they must never do. They apply to
every world and game; a game brief may add its own. The research behind them
is at the end. Status and next steps are in `PROJECT_PLAN.md`.

## Faithful to the original

- **True to the cartoon's character:** its look, pacing, humour, music and
  emotional tone. The aim is to bring back the affection people have for
  these worlds, and to help them understand the worlds better.
- **Inspired by the original, not copied from it.** Original sounds and
  melodies appear only as occasional easter eggs.
- **Restraint.** Modern touches (responsive music, a changing sky, small
  details, expressive characters, things that react) add depth only if the
  game stays quiet, unhurried and recognisable. Study the original closely
  enough to know when to hold back.
- **Beautiful to look at, never loud or busy.** Avoid effects that only
  distract.

## A little world, not a game to finish

- **A world to live in for a while.** The child can wander, look, repeat a
  favourite thing many times, or do nothing. There is no right pace, and
  nothing has to be completed.
- **Time is the same for everyone.** How long the day lasts never depends on
  how much is done. A child who spends the whole day watching a snail gets as
  long as one who tries everything. Curiosity leads to discoveries, never to
  an earlier bedtime.
- **Play needn't teach.** Humour, surprise, repetition and make-believe are
  reason enough. A bowl stuck on Reksio's head is worth having because it is
  funny.
- **Mistakes are safe, and sometimes funny.** No failure, no scores, no
  judging how well the child does.
- **Kindness happens in the story.** Characters help each other: Reksio
  helps, and sometimes he needs help. These are small stories, not lessons
  about being good.
- **Things from the child's own life.** Use objects a child knows from home,
  such as Eliot's grandfather's wall clocks, even if he doesn't know what
  they are for yet.

## Easy to finish

- **The same day every time:** morning, day, evening, sleep. Because the
  routine is predictable, the end is expected, not imposed.
- **The ending is part of the story.** Reksio is tired and goes to sleep;
  there is no limit set from outside the game. Eliot already accepts this.
- **No countdowns or hurry.** A clock in the world tells the time; it never
  says time is running out, and nothing is lost when the day ends. No urgent
  music, flashing or warnings near the end.
- **A new play starts only when a parent chooses.** No "play again" prompt,
  and a toddler's tap can't restart it. Restarting is something only a grown
  up would do.
- **Never designed to keep a child playing.** No streaks, rewards for
  playing longer, things to collect, characters who ask the child to stay or
  come back, ads, or purchases. A short game a parent and child remember
  together is a success; a long session isn't.
- **What success looks like:** laughter, the child trying things on their
  own, curiosity, things to talk about, play that carries on away from the
  screen, and an ending accepted calmly.

## Made to be played together

- **A parent beside the child is the expected way to play.** The games have
  no words, so the talking is the parent's: moments worth pointing at, asking
  about, laughing at together.
- **For parents, a way back to their own childhood:** the cartoon, and the
  episode a gag comes from.

## Is a few minutes a day fine for a toddler?

The question: is a game like this (a few minutes a day, a child of about 3,
usually with a parent) fine to give him? Researched 2026-10-13. Short answer:
yes, if it stays short, calm, free of manipulation, and played together, and
not just before bed. What the sources say, then what follows for us.

### What the guidance says

- **WHO (2019), children under 5:** for ages 2 and 3–4, "sedentary screen
  time should be no more than 1 hour; less is better", and when sitting,
  "reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged". For 1-year-olds
  it is not recommended. ([WHO news release, 24 April
  2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more);
  the full guideline is ISBN 9789241550536.)
- **American Academy of Pediatrics, "Media and Young Minds" (Pediatrics
  138(5), e20162591, 2016), ages 2–5:** "limit screen use to 1 hour per day
  of high-quality programming, coview with your children, help children
  understand what they are seeing, and help them apply what they learn to the
  world around them." Also: "Letting children use media by themselves should
  be avoided"; "Avoid fast-paced programs …, apps with lots of distracting
  content, and any violent content"; "No screens 1 hour before bedtime"; and
  "Avoid using media as the only way to calm your child." It asks makers of
  apps for interfaces "that are not distracting, and that promote shared
  parent–child media use", and to remove advertising. (Read in full.)
- **UK Chief Medical Officers (2019):** the evidence does not show screen use
  causing mental health problems, but that is not evidence of no effect; they
  advise caution and set no time limit, because screen time varies in
  quality, content and effect. ([CMO commentary](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/777026/UK_CMO_commentary_on_screentime_and_social_media_map_of_reviews.pdf);
  read through summaries.)

A 5-minute day is about a twelfth of the WHO and AAP hour.

### What the research says

- **Quality and playing together count, not just minutes.** A meta-analysis
  of 42 studies (about 18,900 children) found more screen time linked to
  weaker language, and educational programmes and watching with a carer
  linked to better language. The links are small and correlational, the
  evidence was rated low quality, and most of the studies are about TV, not
  tablets. (Madigan et al., JAMA Pediatrics 174(7):665–675, 2020; abstract
  and summaries.)
- **Most apps for 3–5-year-olds are built to keep them playing.** Of 133
  apps used by 160 children aged 3–5, 80% had at least one manipulative
  design: characters pressing the child to keep playing, countdown time
  pressure, lures, menus that steer, ads. Only 26 apps had none. (Radesky et
  al., JAMA Network Open 5(6):e2217641, 2022; read in full.) Leaving all of
  that out is what most sets a game like this apart.
- **Endings go best when the screen itself ends.** In interviews with 27
  families and a diary study of 28 more (children 1–5), most transitions away
  from screens went fine (59% neutral, 19% positive, 22% upset). They went
  better with routines, natural stopping points, and when the technology
  ended the session rather than the parent. A parent's two-minute warning
  made children *more* upset. Autoplay and suggested videos caused many of
  the fights. (Hiniker et al., CHI 2016, "Screen Time Tantrums"; [UW
  News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2016/05/05/two-minute-warnings-make-kids-screen-time-tantrums-worse/)
  and the lab's page.) This matches what we saw with Eliot: he accepts
  Reksio going to sleep.
- **Fantastical, fast content can briefly dull self-control.** After 9–20
  minutes of fast, fantastical cartoons (physically impossible events),
  4-year-olds did worse on executive function tasks than after slow,
  realistic shows, drawing or play. In the 2015 follow-up, fantasy mattered
  more than pace. These are short-term effects in small laboratory studies.
  An unpublished thesis that tried the same with tablet games did not find
  the effect. (Lillard & Peterson, Pediatrics 128(4):644–649, 2011; Lillard
  et al., Developmental Psychology 51(6):792–805, 2015; abstracts only.)
- **Screens and sleep.** In preschoolers, heavy screen use goes with shorter
  and poorer sleep, and poorer sleep with more behaviour problems
  (correlational; a review of 13 studies, Merín et al., European Child &
  Adolescent Psychiatry, 2024; abstract only).
- **What makes an app worth a child's time:** the child is actively
  thinking (not just tapping), engaged rather than distracted, the play is
  meaningful, and it is shared with others. (Hirsh-Pasek et al., Psychological
  Science in the Public Interest 16(1):3–34, 2015; summaries only.)

### What follows for us (our reading)

- **A short day suits a toddler.** 5 minutes is well within the guidance. A
  day of about 5–8 minutes needs no limit beyond the game's own ending.
- **The ending is designed to be a natural stopping point.** Reksio's
  bedtime, the same every play, with no autoplay and no "next", is what the
  transitions research says works. It must not become a warning: a cuckoo
  each hour is part of the world, not a countdown. Whether a visible evening
  calms Eliot or sounds like "two more minutes" isn't known; watch him play.
- **Play together.** Both the AAP and the research favour a parent beside the
  child. The game should give the parent things to point at and laugh at, and
  README should say so, not present the game as something to hand a child to
  play alone.
- **Not a bedtime story.** Reksio's bedtime could invite using the game to
  settle a child before bed, which the AAP advises against (no screens in the
  hour before bed). Play is for the daytime; the evening in the game is
  Reksio's, not the child's. README says so.
- **Not a way to calm a child.** Don't present the game as one.
- **Gentle slapstick, little magic.** Reksio's gags are physical comedy with
  a dog in a real yard, which keeps us on the slow, realistic side of the
  Lillard studies. Keep impossible events rare. (Those studies are about
  TV, and they measured only the time just after watching.)
- **No manipulation at all,** including the subtle kinds: characters asking
  the child to stay, things to collect, rewards for coming back, time
  pressure.
- **Not an educational app,** and we make no claim that it teaches. Its worth
  is shared laughter and a calm ending, which none of these studies measure.
  Only watching families play will tell.
